RESEARCH QUESTION AND DESIGN ARGUMENT

How can interactive storytelling represent the everyday communication
challenges experienced by bilingual speakers, particularly in translation,
cultural interpretation, and language-based identity? Between Two Languages
approaches this question through a fictional student, An, whose family and
classmates meet around a course project. The player does not translate a
list of vocabulary items. Instead, the player chooses what an answer should
do in a particular relationship: convey information, invite participation,
provide context, ask for time, or establish a boundary.

The game's design argument is that multiple reasonable responses can reveal
different aspects of a situation. A literal translation can preserve an
image while confusing a listener. A paraphrase can make the practical point
clear while leaving some interpersonal meaning unexplained. A longer
explanation can create understanding while requiring time and effort. A
choice-based story makes those tradeoffs available for comparison without
requiring a single correct answer. This is an argument about representation,
not a measured claim about what the player learns.

EPISODE 1: THE SOCIAL WORK OF AN ORDINARY QUESTION

The first episode takes place at dinner. Mom asks whether An has eaten, and
Jules asks for a translation. The three options stay close to the reference
plan: a rendering about rice, an everyday question about eating, and an
explanation of checking on someone. Each branch continues beyond the first
response. This is essential because a misunderstanding is not necessarily
the end of communication. Speakers can ask, correct, laugh, and gesture.

Baker and Jones's listed distinction between word-level and pragmatic
equivalence offers a starting vocabulary for analyzing these choices [3].
The prototype's application is deliberately situated: Mom's actions, the
time of day, and the relationship explain this particular question. The
script does not claim that one sentence always expresses care. The same
approach shapes the greeting scene, where Mom names her preferred form of
address. An can approximate it in English, preserve it, or ask about it.
The person being addressed participates in the decision.

The final dinner scene introduces a boundary. Jules is full and wants to
decline more food. Every option preserves that refusal; the choices concern
how to express it. This prevents the game's premise from turning cultural
interpretation into permission to override a person. It also provides a
positive exchange in which understanding does not require perfect speech.

EPISODE 2: FINDING EXPRESSION WITHOUT A DEFICIT MODEL

At school, An thinks of ngại while considering a request to record dinner.
The player can use an approximate English word, describe the situation, or
introduce the Vietnamese word and explain its intended sense. An is fluent,
but fluency does not require a perfectly satisfying label at every moment.
The dialogue also gives Minh a different interpretation, so shared language
does not become automatic access to another person's thoughts.

Grosjean's abstract argues against treating a bilingual speaker as two
monolinguals contained in one person [1]. In the game, this is a reason to
avoid a meter that rewards complete equivalence across languages. It is
not evidence that An's pause has a particular cognitive cause. The context
note explicitly recognizes that word searching also occurs in monolingual
speech. A later decision lets the player request time, collaborate, or use
an example rather than accept an inaccurate word offered by a friend.

Gumperz's summaries place switching within conversational interpretation
[2]. The corresponding scene involves a joke between An and Minh. The joke
works before Jules understands it. The player then chooses a bridge: share
its image, give its practical point, or invite Minh to explain. Switching
is neither punished nor treated as automatically inclusive. The relevant
question is how this group can make participation possible in that moment.

EPISODE 3: AUDIENCE, IDENTITY, AND THE RIGHT TO PRIVACY

The final episode connects the family and school settings. An can describe
the project to Mom through its academic question, through a familiar sound,
or after finishing the dishes. These alternatives are all truthful. They
show how the timing and emphasis of an account can change with its audience
without turning one version into a false self.

Bucholtz and Hall's abstract frames identity as produced in interaction,
with attention to roles, relationships, and local positions [4]. The game
uses this as a prompt to resist a simple division into an English self and
a Vietnamese self. When Jules asks whether An feels like a different person,
the player can describe continuity, describe a personal difference, or keep
the question private. No option awards authenticity. In each case, Jules
responds to the answer An actually gives.

The endings emphasize connection, personal agency, or listening. They do
not resolve all the character's experiences. Instead, each returns to an
ordinary moment: a longer conversation at home, a decision about sharing a
recording, or the sound of a chair being pulled out. The player ends with
a journal of specific exchanges rather than a diagnosis of their identity.

WHY INTERACTION, AND WHY THIS SCOPE?

The repository abstract for Tanenbaum and Tanenbaum offers agency as a
commitment to meaning rather than simply the number of available actions
[5]. This provides a tentative rationale for local branches that rejoin.
The game has nine decisions and 27 distinct reaction sequences. It does
not need thousands of separately written endings to show what a response
conveys. The journal makes the selected interpretation visible. Backtracking
lets the player compare another response and updates the journal accordingly.

Isbister's publisher description and excerpt connect game design with
emotional experiences and the consequences of choices [6]. The prototype
uses small reactions rather than spectacle: someone relaxes after a refusal
is understood, a friend waits for a thought, or a joke becomes shared. Those
are design intentions. Establishing that players feel empathy, understand
bilingualism differently, or retain concepts would require an evaluation
that has not been conducted.

IMPLEMENTATION, LIMITATIONS, AND NEXT STEPS

The game uses native HTML controls, CSS, JavaScript, and three JSON episode
files. Separating prose from rendering lets the author replace fictional
examples with appropriate experiences without changing the controller.
Translations, contextual explanations, keyboard navigation, and a readable
transcript reduce barriers to participation. Automated checks validate every
choice path, saving and resuming, backtracking, and controller behavior.
They do not establish cultural accuracy or certify accessibility.
